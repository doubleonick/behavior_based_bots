Prologue, draft 3 (a proposal)

Your story and your sketches, laid out as three spreads. Your words are kept wherever they worked; the new parts are the bridge from the cell to Val and the quest at the end. Underlined: the five words to learn here. The orange notes are for you, not the child.

Billions of years ago, Earth was a planet of dull grey rock.

Then, CRASH! Another young world slammed right into it.

i

The crash boiled Earth's oceans away. Deep inside, Earth grew hotter and began to churn.

Pieces of the broken world flew out into space, and slowly gathered into something new: the Moon.

ii
What changed (spread 1)
  • The crash gets its own beat ("Then, CRASH!"), a page turn's worth of drama.
  • "Its core became hotter and more dynamic" becomes "deep inside, Earth grew hotter and began to churn": something a child can picture.
  • "Earth also gained a moon. The Moon." becomes one sentence, with the Moon in bold.

Slowly, the new Earth cooled. Rain fell for a very, very long time, and new oceans filled.

In that warm water, some molecules could do something amazing: make copies of themselves. First RNA, then DNA. They are the chemical codes of life.

iii

The copies were never quite perfect. Most tiny mistakes, called mutations, changed nothing. A few made things worse. And a few made things better.

Somewhere in those oceans, a thin wall wrapped around some of these copying molecules and kept the rest of the ocean out. That wall turned them into the first true living things: cells.

Hi! I'm alive! I can keep myself going… but I can't go anywhere I choose. Not yet.

iv
What changed (spread 2)
  • Cooling and rain explain where the new oceans came from.
  • Mutations come back from draft 1, in three short sentences (most change nothing), because they matter again at the selection stop.
  • "The first agreed upon life" becomes "the first true living things: cells".
  • Your "Hi!" cell finishes its thought. That line is the bridge: it brings in agency through the character, before the word appears.

It took life a very long time to learn to feel the world around it, to move through it, and to choose where to go.

How did it happen? To find out, we need a very special explorer.

This is Val Brooks, named after two scientists who built machines to understand living things.

v

Val is a robot. Val is not alive. And, like that first cell, Val starts with almost nothing: no senses, no way to choose where to go.

But Val can be built, one step at a time, the way life built itself.

Living things that can act on the world, and not just float along in it, are called agents. An agent can:

  • navigate
  • communicate
  • manipulate

Can something that isn't alive become an agent?

Let's find out, and maybe learn something about you, too.

What changed (spread 3)
  • A problem comes before the explorer ("It took life a very long time…"), so Val arrives for a reason.
  • "Named after ___" is filled at kid level (Valentino Braitenberg and Rodney Brooks, if that is who you meant; their full names belong in the Handbook).
  • Val's start matches the cell's, which leads straight into Meet Val ("can't feel or move yet").
  • The three stacked questions become one question and a checklist: your three verbs, with empty boxes the stops can tick (stickers?). "Does all life have agency?" can return as a Wonder question at a later stop. Add "sense" as a fourth box if you want Vehicle 1 to earn the first tick.
  • "About YOU!" stays, as the last beat before the map.
